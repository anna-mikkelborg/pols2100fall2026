Generalizability

  • From Week 4: theories should explain as broad a class of phenomena as possible
  • When we do experiments, we learn about the effect of the particular treatment studied…
    • Among the people the experiment was conducted among…
    • At the time/in the context in which the experiment was conducted.
  • But we often want to know if the treatment would work beyond this narrow scope!

Examples

  • Treatment: Problem Set 3 looks at the effect of a “gentle” social pressure mailer. What if we made it even more subtle?
  • People: Would the effects of the “both” condition among Utah Republican precinct chairs be similar if we tested it on Democratic precinct chairs? Republican chairs in other states?
  • Time/context: The social pressure experiment was conducted in an off-year primary election. Would the effects be different in a presidential general election?
  • With the last two, we are effectively asking how heterogeneous we would expect treatment effects to be.

Why might effects not generalize?

  • Imagine an experiment done in the 2020 election in Wyoming and Wisconsin
  • By 2020, social pressure mailers were very common — swing state voters (like Wisconsinites) were receiving a lot of them

Evaluating genalizability

  • Meta-analysis: looking across the results of many similar experiments to see how the results vary.
  • An analysis of analyses!
  • Only works with questions that have been studied multiple times in different contexts

Campaign persuasion meta-analysis

Codebook

Variable Description
Experiment Paper reference of the experiment
effect Estimate of the effect size
SE Standard error of the effect estimate
precision Precision of the estimate, defined as 1 / SE2 (used as weights)
General 1 if the experiment took place in a general election, 0 if not
Primary 1 if the experiment took place in a primary election, 0 if not (primary elections typically receive less attention)
Treatment.within.2months.of.election 1 if the experiment took place within 2 months of election day, 0 if more than 2 months before

Meta-analysis using weighted averages

Why weight

  • When we conduct meta-analyses, we weight our estimates by how precise they are (i.e., how little noise they have)
Experiment Estimate Standard Error 95% Confidence
Interval
Experiment 1: Letter A 8% 30% [-50%, 66%]
Experiment 2: Letter B 5% 1% [3%, 7%]
  • We want to weight the experiment with the smaller standard error more heavily!

Calculating precision weights

\(precision = \frac{1}{\text{Standard Error}^2}\)

The weighted.mean() command

Basic recipe:

weighted.mean(dataset.name$main.variable.name, dataset.name$weighting.variable.name)

  • Data weights are used in many applications!
  • e.g., to make campaign polls more representative…
  • In this class, weights will always be provided for you

Understanding why effects vary

Why are the effects of campaign persuasion larger in primaries than in general elections?

  • Party loyalty?
  • Campaign spending?
  • Meta-analyses can prompt us to develop more nuanced theories and hypotheses!
  • We can then design new studies to test those theories
    • E.g., do a survey experiment where we manipulate whether voters receive party information.

Summing up

  • The best theories are general
  • So we hope our empirical findings will generalize
    • To other, similar treatments
    • To other people/groups/units
    • To other places/contexts
  • Meta-analyses help us test this
  • Precision-weighting accounts for varying levels of uncertainty across studies
